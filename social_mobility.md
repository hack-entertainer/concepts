# Social Mobility Framework

## Core Observation: Structural Attribution Bias

Western society is often described as exhibiting *survivorship bias* toward successful individuals — the idea that we draw false conclusions because we only see winners. But the mechanism at work is deeper than that. Classic survivorship bias is **epistemological** — a failure of perception. What operates here is **structural** — society doesn't just observe winners, it actively rewards their networks. The bias isn't in how we see the world; it's baked into how resources flow through it.

The more precise frame is **attribution bias**: success is attributed to individual qualities — talent, discipline, work ethic — while inherited advantages are systematically discounted or rendered invisible. This does two things simultaneously: it celebrates winners as deserving, and implicitly frames those who don't succeed as personally lacking. The narrative provides ideological cover for the mechanism, making it self-concealing and durable across generations.

That advantage transfers not just as wealth, but as:

- **Cultural capital** — knowing how to navigate institutions
- **Social capital** — inherited networks and connections
- **Symbolic capital** — the legitimacy that comes from association with success

The system produces inequality passively — it doesn't need to actively exclude anyone, it simply continues doing what it does. And because poverty and underemployment are quite *visible* in modern society — merely explained away rather than hidden — survivorship bias isn't quite the right diagnosis. The failures aren't invisible. They're just misattributed.

## The Intervention Model: Targeted Mobility Pathways

The goal is not to redistribute existing social capital or dismantle existing hierarchies. Instead, the model creates **on-ramps that didn't exist before** — pathways calibrated to a person's starting position rather than assuming a level playing field.

This is not zero-sum. Moving someone from chronic underemployment into stable work adds to society's productive base without diminishing anyone else's position. It is better understood as **targeted infrastructure** — building roads to places that currently have none.

## Strategic Principles

### 1. Triage
Identify the most accessible interventions first. Prioritize where the intervention-to-outcome ratio is highest. This is an engineering question, not an ideological one — and far more tractable than attempting to solve everything at once.

### 2. Individualized Progress Records
Treat participants as trajectories, not categories. A personal progress record reveals who is *moving*, even slowly, under their own effort. That person is the most valuable recruit — internal motivation cannot be installed by any program. The role of intervention is to remove external friction.

### 3. Recruit at the Margins
The person at the edge of a poverty or crime cycle — inconsistently employed, one crisis away from stability — requires far less intervention to cross the threshold into self-sustainability. Each person who crosses becomes a proof of concept and a potential bridge for others behind them.

### 4. Chip at the Edges
Target the periphery of big problems rather than their center. Early successes generate credibility, data, and role models within the community itself — worth more than any external messaging campaign. Shrinking the edges gradually reduces the gravitational pull of the cycle on those still inside it.

## Operational Architecture

### Small Wins Over Big Swings
A series of consistent small wins builds more reliably toward larger outcomes than training for infrequent, high-stakes efforts. Small wins:

- Maintain agent motivation and competence
- Build tacit, community-specific knowledge that compounds over time
- Yield data when they fail, rather than demoralization

### Discretionary, Non-Emergency Action
Because intervention is not crisis-driven, agents can be selective, patient, and methodical. They can disengage from situations that aren't ready without catastrophic consequence. This freedom to be strategic is rare in social work and almost never fully exploited.

### Regular Strategy Review
Scheduled review prevents drift toward what *feels* good rather than what *works*. It forces honest accounting — which recruits progressed, which interventions landed, where resources were wasted — and builds a grounded, context-specific evidence base over time.

---

## Why This Works

This framework functions as a **self-improving learning organization** rather than a traditional charity or government program. It is:

- **Too pragmatic to be easily politicized** — it makes no ideological claims
- **Resilient to failure** — small failures are instructive, not catastrophic
- **Compounding by design** — each success reduces friction for the next
- **Adaptive** — regular review allows strategy to follow evidence honestly

The measured pace and discretionary nature mean the system can scale what works and quietly discard what doesn't — building durable, replicable change from the edges inward.
